# Khaerzulveth

> The mask was carved from a sleeping face. Its owner woke somewhere else.

## The Theater beneath the Empty Moon

Khaerzulveth stands at the edge of a plain where moonlight falls without a moon. Its theater has no doors. Visitors enter by following the sound of applause until they discover a seat beneath them and a stage before their eyes.

The seats are numbered in a language that can be read only while standing. Once seated, spectators remember different numbers, often those of houses they inhabited as children. Their neighbors remain visible, but the distance between the seats changes with every breath.

Upon the stage rests a white mask. Behind it hangs a dark curtain, stitched with scales that occasionally turn toward the audience. The mask casts the shadow of a creature whose wings are too wide for the theater.

## The Actor Who Arrived after His Bow

The first performance began with a bow. Nobody stood upon the stage. A shadow leaned forward, and the audience applauded before anyone could decide whose gesture they had witnessed.

An actor then appeared wearing the white mask. His hands were gloved, his voice quiet, and his cloak moved as though something beneath it were unfolding. He spoke one line: the face has been returned to the place where it was borrowed.

A spectator recognized the voice of her brother, who had never visited the theater. Another recognized his own voice at an age he had not yet reached. A third heard only the movement of wings and began crying without knowing which part of the performance had saddened him.

The actor bowed again. This time, his shadow remained upright. Behind the curtain, something applauded with a sound like claws striking glass.

## The Program with an Unwritten Title

Every spectator finds a program beneath the seat. Its pages list an empty title, three acts, and a cast consisting entirely of names the reader has forgotten.

The first act is described as a room. The second is described as the person who left it. The third contains no description, only a small drawing of a mask placed upon a chair.

A visitor attempted to write the missing title. His ink gathered into a dark circle and sank through the paper. Below his seat, a small opening appeared in the floor, revealing another theater where the audience wore masks and the stage held an unoccupied chair.

He covered the opening with the program. By the end of the performance, the circle had become a name he almost recognized. He left without taking the paper, but found the same name written upon the inside of his gloves.

## A Wardrobe of Unclaimed Shapes

Beyond the curtain stands a wardrobe that cannot be approached from the stage. Its interior is visible only in polished objects held by members of the audience: a ring, a buckle, the back of a spoon.

The wardrobe contains cloaks shaped for unfamiliar bodies. One has a collar wide enough for two heads. Another has sleeves that continue into folded wings. A third remains empty even when someone seems to be standing inside it.

The theater's attendant carries a key of pale horn. He never opens the wardrobe. Instead, he places the key upon whichever seat has failed to acquire a spectator.

Sometimes the key disappears. Sometimes a cloak emerges in its reflection, warm and damp, with a white mask hidden beneath its collar. The attendant returns the key to his pocket and leaves that seat unnumbered until morning.

## The Words along the Mask's Teeth

**Khaer — zul — veth — eiru.**

The mask has no visible mouth, but these sounds appear along its inner edge when a lamp is held behind it. Their letters resemble teeth arranged around a space too narrow for a human face.

An actor tried to pronounce them before the performance. The theater's seats turned toward the curtain. He spoke the final sound, and every spectator saw a pale dragon reflected in the mask's smooth surface.

The dragon was sleeping with its head upon a stage. Around it, empty chairs formed a circle. In one chair sat a small masked figure, holding a lamp toward the creature's closed eye.

The actor lowered the lamp. The seats returned to their positions. Nobody could remember whether the performance had already begun.

## Applause from the Other Side

> Who receives the bow before the actor arrives?
>
> The face that has not finished waking.
>
> Why does the curtain turn its scales toward us?
>
> Because someone behind it is listening with a borrowed body.
>
> Which seat should remain empty?
>
> The one whose number you remember without standing.

These exchanges were heard during the third act. The attendant wrote them on the back of a program, leaving narrow spaces where the speakers' names should have appeared.

By morning, the spaces contained drawings of chairs. Each chair held the same white mask. None cast a shadow.

## The Performance without a Final Bow

The theater still receives spectators beneath the empty moon. Its attendant collects programs after every performance, except those that have begun to grow warm beneath the seats.

The actor's name remains absent from the cast. The mask rests upon the stage when nobody wears it, and its wide shadow continues reaching beyond the curtain toward a wardrobe nobody has opened.

Khaerzulveth waits inside the applause that precedes a gesture. Somewhere in a smaller theater, a sleeping creature turns its face toward a lamp and begins remembering the voice that was borrowed from it.
